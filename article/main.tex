\title{From Corpus Evidence to Controlled Generation:\\
A Human-in-the-Loop Approach for Low-Resource Languages}
\author{Fabrício Ferraz Gerardi}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
This paper presents an evidence-bound architecture for controlled sentence
generation in a low-resource language, using Boe-Bororo as a case study. The
central methodological distinction is between corpus observation and
grammatical licensing. Corpus forms, frequencies, dependency annotations, and
translations are treated as evidence for analysis, but do not automatically
license generation. Generation is instead constrained by human-reviewed coding
frames, person-index realizations, construction-specific morphology, and
morphotactic restrictions. A first frozen experimental baseline evaluates five
lexemes across monovalent, extended-intransitive, and divalent frames. The
system generates all 28 requested reviewed structural cells, while none of the
complete generated sentences is an exact corpus sentence. Fourteen unique
generated predicate forms are independently attested as corpus tokens. The
baseline therefore separates controlled recombination from simple retrieval
while preserving a strict distinction between attestation and grammatical
analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Generative experiments with low-resource languages face a basic evidential
problem: occurrence in a corpus is not equivalent to a grammatical rule, while
absence from a small corpus is not evidence of ungrammaticality. This problem
becomes more acute when automatic induction or a large language model is allowed
to convert distributional regularities directly into productive analyses.

We investigate an alternative architecture in which documentary corpus evidence
and grammatical licensing remain formally distinct. The CorBo corpus supplies
observations. Human linguistic review establishes the morphological and
constructional facts that may license generation. A deterministic generator then
realizes only reviewed combinations, retaining provenance for the evidence and
rules used. Artificial-intelligence components can subsequently propose
structures for evaluation, but do not themselves constitute grammatical
evidence.

The contribution is methodological rather than a claim to broad-coverage
generation. We ask whether a small, explicitly reviewed grammatical layer can
support reproducible controlled generation while making the boundary between
observed data, linguistic analysis, and generated output inspectable.

\section{Boe-Bororo and the corpus setting}
\label{sec:bororo}

Bororo is a Bororoan language spoken in Mato Grosso, Brazil. The grammatical
description underlying the present system is based especially on data from
speakers in Meruri. Contemporary Bororo communities are sociolinguistically
heterogeneous: their historical trajectories differ, and speaker competence
varies by generation and locality. Consequently, variation in documentary
materials cannot be interpreted independently of locality, chronology, contact,
mobility, and language shift.

This point also affects the treatment of historical documentation. Sources
produced in different periods and contact settings are not interchangeable
samples of a single invariant linguistic system. Historical records and modern
fieldwork differ in locality, transcription practice, consultant background,
genre, and documentary purpose. The controlled-generation experiment therefore
does not collapse all documentary forms into a single automatically productive
grammar.

The present experiment uses a reviewed subset of CorBo represented in CoNLL-U.
The corpus functions as the observational layer of the system. Token forms,
lemmas, UPOS labels, dependency relations, sentence contexts, and translations
may be retrieved for human inspection, but corpus frequency or formal
resemblance does not promote a morphological analysis to reviewed status. The
trusted experimental subset is deliberately separated from larger working
collections whose annotation remains under development.

This separation is important for reproducibility. The generator consumes a
specified corpus snapshot, while grammatical licenses are stored independently
and require explicit human review. Thus a change in corpus annotation can alter
the observational evidence without silently changing the grammar used for
generation.

\section{Corpus evidence is not grammatical licensing}
\label{sec:evidence}

The architecture distinguishes three evidential layers. First,
\emph{reviewed grammar} records human-reviewed coding frames, person-index
cells, stem-class assignments where a complete paradigm is independently
supported, and construction-specific cells. Second, the \emph{generated
candidate} records the surface output and the rules used to produce it. Third,
\emph{corpus evidence} records whether a generated surface or predicate form
occurs in the supplied corpus. The third layer never promotes itself into the
first.

This distinction also applies to lexical identity. Homographic corpus forms are
not automatically assigned to the same lexical item, and a familiar-looking
prefix or suffix is not segmented solely from its shape. Review queues expose
the relevant contexts while leaving person, segmentation, stem class, and
coding frame unset until they are explicitly reviewed.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Recent work on endangered-language NLP provides an important comparison for
the present architecture. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022cherokee} frame
computational work on Cherokee around collaboration with language communities
and propose machine-in-the-loop methods for enriching resources. Joshi
et al.~\cite{joshi2019unsung} likewise emphasize that low-resource language
technology is not only a modeling problem: data creation, deployment, and the
conditions under which a technology is useful to a community are integral to
the task.

NüshuRescue provides a particularly close computational contrast
\cite{yang2025nushurescue}. Yang et al.\ construct a 500-sentence manually
validated Nüshu--Chinese corpus and use 35 seed examples with GPT-4-Turbo to
generate additional translations. Their pipeline combines LLM generation,
automatic validation, and manual review. A language-specific length constraint
raises their reported strict translation accuracy from 31.37\% without the
constraint to 48.69\% with it. The study therefore provides direct evidence
that explicit linguistic constraints can materially improve an LLM-based
low-resource pipeline.

The present work shares the broader human-in-the-loop motivation but addresses
a different problem. NüshuRescue uses an LLM primarily to expand parallel data
and seeks to reduce the amount of manual annotation required. Our system does
not treat generated data as a route to automatic grammar expansion. Instead,
human-reviewed linguistic analysis defines the space within which generation is
licensed. The LLM, when introduced, is positioned upstream as a proposal
mechanism; it cannot promote its own output into grammatical knowledge.

This contrast is consequential for evaluation. Translation accuracy measures
agreement with a reference translation. Controlled grammatical generation
requires additional questions: whether a person realization was independently
reviewed, whether a coding frame licenses the requested argument structure,
whether construction-specific morphology has been generalized beyond its
evidence, and whether corpus attestation is being mistaken for grammatical
productivity. Our evaluation therefore reports provenance and licensing
coverage separately from surface attestation.

\section{Methodological motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

The design addresses a recurrent asymmetry in low-resource computational work.
A corpus can show that a form occurred in a particular documented context, but
a generator requires a stronger decision: whether a representation is licensed
for productive use in a specified construction. The latter decision may depend
on lexical identity, coding frame, person-index allomorphy, morphotactics, and
construction-specific restrictions that cannot safely be recovered from token
frequency alone.

We therefore distinguish \emph{observation}, \emph{analysis}, and
\emph{licensing}. Observation records what the corpus contains. Analysis is a
human linguistic claim about the structure of that evidence. Licensing is an
explicit computational decision that a reviewed analysis is sufficiently
specific to support a generation operation. These states are related, but they
are not collapsed into one another.

This distinction is especially consequential when paradigms are incomplete.
An attested person form can license an exact cell without establishing a full
stem class. Likewise, a form reviewed inside an imperative, subjunctive,
gerundial, negative, or irrealis construction does not automatically establish
the corresponding ordinary indicative form. The system represents such partial
knowledge directly rather than completing paradigms by analogy.

\section{Evidence-first architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The current pipeline is:

\begin{quote}
CorBo $\rightarrow$ corpus evidence $\rightarrow$ human linguistic review
$\rightarrow$ reviewed grammar $\rightarrow$ controlled generator
$\rightarrow$ generated candidate $\rightarrow$ validation and corpus
attestation.
\end{quote}

The architecture is deliberately asymmetric. Corpus evidence can motivate a
human review decision, but cannot directly license a new generation rule.
Likewise, corpus attestation of a generated predicate form is reported as
observational support and not as proof that the complete generated structural
cell is attested.

\section{Reviewed grammatical constraints}
\label{sec:grammar}

The baseline distinguishes monovalent, extended-intransitive, and divalent
coding frames. Morphological licensing is also separated into two types.
A \texttt{full\_stem\_class} license permits the independently reviewed
person-index paradigm associated with that lexical assignment. An
\texttt{exact\_person\_cell} license permits only the explicitly reviewed
lemma--person realization.

Construction-specific morphology is stored separately from the ordinary person
paradigm. This prevents an imperative, subjunctive, gerund, optative, negative,
or irrealis form from silently licensing an ordinary person cell. The current
morphotactic model additionally records the reviewed complementary distribution
of \textit{-re}, \textit{-wo}, \textit{-iagu}, \textit{-ie}, and
\textit{-ia} as a mutually exclusive suffix domain. Membership in this domain
does not by itself assign an unrevised functional interpretation to a suffix.

\section{Controlled generation}
\label{sec:generation}

For each lexical item, a reviewed coding frame selects the appropriate
structural generator. Generation fails closed: if a required morphological or
constructional license is absent, the requested candidate is blocked rather
than completed by analogy. This is particularly important for partial
paradigms, where individual reviewed cells must not be extrapolated to an
entire stem class.

For divalent predicates, evidence for the predicate form concerns the indexed
O realization and does not independently establish the A$\times$O structural
cell. Extended-intransitive predicates similarly keep the core predicate and
additional oblique participant distinct; no postposition is generalized across
the construction without lexical evidence.

\section{Experimental baseline}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We froze a first experimental baseline after resolving the human-review queue
for five target lexemes: \textit{nudu}, \textit{meru}, \textit{kodu},
\textit{mako}, and \textit{maku}. The evaluation requests only structural cells
licensed by the reviewed grammar and compares generated surfaces against the
supplied trusted corpus.

Of the 28 requested cells, 16 belong to three monovalent lexemes, six to one
extended-intransitive lexeme, and six to one divalent lexeme. All requested
cells were generated and none was blocked. Twenty of the 28 records
(71.4\%) depend on exact reviewed person cells, whereas eight (28.6\%) are
licensed through a complete reviewed stem-class assignment.

No complete generated sentence is an exact normalized corpus sentence. At the
same time, 14 unique generated predicate forms occur independently as corpus
tokens. These two measurements answer different questions: sentence
attestation detects exact retrieval-like overlap, whereas predicate-form
attestation supplies morphological corpus evidence. Neither measurement is
treated as automatic grammatical validation.

\section{Evaluation design and limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The baseline is intentionally small. It measures the behavior of an
evidence-bound generation architecture, not general coverage of Bororo.
Predicate-form occurrence is morphological evidence only; in particular, for a
divalent predicate it does not resolve the A argument of the generated
structural cell. Conversely, a generated form absent from the supplied corpus
is not thereby classified as ungrammatical.

The present numbers should therefore be read as reproducible coverage and
evidence measures, not as an accuracy or grammaticality score. Broader
evaluation will require additional reviewed lexical items and independent human
judgment of novel outputs.

\section{AI as a proposal layer}
\label{sec:ai}

A subsequent experiment will add an AI proposal layer above the deterministic
grammar. Rather than asking a language model to produce an unrestricted Bororo
surface sentence, the model can propose an abstract request such as a lemma,
coding frame, participant features, and construction. The reviewed generator
then realizes or rejects that request. This preserves the separation between a
probabilistic proposal mechanism and the evidence required to license a
surface form.

A direct comparison between unrestricted model output and model proposals
filtered through the controlled system can then measure constraint violations,
blocked proposals, and the provenance available for accepted candidates. These
results are not included in the v1 baseline.

\section{Discussion}

The baseline demonstrates the practical consequence of separating evidence
from licensing. Most generated records depend on individually reviewed
morphological cells rather than paradigm-wide extrapolation. The architecture
therefore exposes incompleteness instead of hiding it behind automatic analogy.
This is useful in a documentary setting, where a conservative unresolved state
can be more informative than a fluent but unsupported output.

The approach also makes incremental revision possible. New corpus observations
can enter a review queue without changing the grammar; a human decision can
then add an exact cell, a construction-specific realization, or, where
independently justified, a complete lexical class. Each change can be tested
against the existing reviewed inventory.

\section{Conclusion}

Controlled generation for a low-resource language need not begin by treating
corpus distributions or language-model output as grammar. The Bororo case study
shows an alternative workflow in which corpus evidence, human analysis,
generation, and attestation remain separate computational objects. The first
baseline is deliberately narrow, but it provides a reproducible point from
which broader lexical coverage and an AI-assisted proposal experiment can be
evaluated without weakening the evidential boundary on which the system is
based.
